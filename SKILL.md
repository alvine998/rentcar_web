# AI Copywriting

Copy exists to move a specific person to take a specific action. Good copy is clear first, clever second. The most common failure of AI-written copy is not bad grammar, it is blandness: polished sentences that could describe any product to anyone. This skill is about being specific, being human, and being useful to the reader.

## Step 1: Get the brief before writing

Copy written without context is generic by definition. Check what the user has already given you (including pasted URLs, files, or earlier messages), then fill gaps. If critical items are missing, ask at most 3 short questions, grouped in one message. If the request is small (a headline, a caption) and the answer is inferable, write first and state your assumptions in one line.

The brief:

| Item | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Product/offer** | What it is, what it does, price or terms if relevant |
| **Audience** | Who they are, what they already believe, what they're tired of |
| **Goal / action** | Buy, sign up, click, reply, share, remember? One goal per piece |
| **Key proof** | Numbers, testimonials, awards, guarantees, real details |
| **Channel & length** | Where it will live and any character/word limits |
| **Voice** | Brand adjectives, example copy they like, words to avoid |

If the user has no proof or specifics, say so and ask. Never invent statistics, testimonials, awards, customer names, or guarantees. Use clearly marked placeholders like `[X% faster, add real number]` instead.

## Step 2: Find the angle

Before drafting, decide the single idea the copy will carry. Ask:

1. What does the reader want, in their words, not the product's?
2. What's the specific pain, friction, or desire that makes this offer relevant *now*?
3. What is true of this product that competitors can't honestly say?

Then pick one angle, such as: a sharp pain point, a surprising benefit, a before/after, a counterintuitive claim, social proof, urgency that is real, or a vivid specific detail. When useful, offer 2-3 distinct angles (not 3 rewordings of the same line) so the user can choose.

## Step 3: Draft using a structure

Use a framework as scaffolding, not a template to fill in mechanically.

- **PAS (Problem, Agitate, Solve):** Name the problem, make the cost of ignoring it felt, present the solution. Best for pain-driven offers, emails, short ads.
- **AIDA (Attention, Interest, Desire, Action):** Hook, build relevance, create want with proof, ask. Best for landing pages and longer pieces.
- **BAB (Before, After, Bridge):** Show life now, life after, and how the product connects them. Best for transformation offers.
- **FAB (Features, Advantages, Benefits):** Turn every feature into what it does for the reader and why that matters. Best for product descriptions.
- **4 Ps (Promise, Picture, Proof, Push):** Make a promise, help them see it, back it up, ask for action.

Always lead with the reader's situation or the payoff, not the company's history. Put the most important thing first; people skim.

## Step 4: Write it like a person

### Principles

- **Specific beats abstract.** "Cuts invoicing from 3 hours to 20 minutes" beats "streamlines your workflow." If you can't be specific, ask for the detail.
- **Benefits over features.** A feature is what it is; a benefit is what changes for the reader. Always connect them.
- **Write to one person.** Use "you." Short sentences. Everyday words. Read it aloud mentally; if you wouldn't say it to a smart friend, rewrite it.
- **One idea, one action.** Every piece has one primary CTA. Make it concrete ("Start your free trial") not vague ("Learn more").
- **Earn every claim.** Pair bold claims with proof or a concrete detail. Unsupported superlatives lose trust.
- **Vary rhythm.** Mix short punchy lines with longer ones. A wall of same-length sentences reads as machine-made.

### Avoid AI-sounding copy

These patterns signal generic AI output. Cut or rewrite them:

- Stock openers: "In today's fast-paced world...", "Are you tired of...?", "Imagine a world where..."
- Hollow buzzwords: *unlock, elevate, supercharge, seamless, revolutionize, game-changer, cutting-edge, leverage, empower, next-level, harness, delve, tapestry, journey*
- The "It's not just X, it's Y" construction and its cousins ("More than a product, it's a movement")
- Rule-of-three lists used by reflex ("fast, easy, and reliable") with no specifics behind them
- Em dash overuse and breathless stacked fragments for fake drama
- Stacked exclamation marks and emoji padding (unless the brand voice calls for them)
- Vague superlatives: "best-in-class," "world-class," "industry-leading" without proof
- Closing with a generic summary or "Don't miss out!" urgency that isn't real

A quick test: swap in a competitor's name. If the sentence still works, it's too generic. Rewrite it with something only this product can claim.

## Step 5: Format for the channel

Match length and structure to where the copy will live.

- **Headlines:** Clear over clever. Aim for under ~10 words. Test types: benefit, curiosity, number/proof, question, command. Provide 5-10 options across different angles when asked for headlines.
- **Subject lines:** Roughly 30-50 characters, front-load the hook, avoid spam triggers (ALL CAPS, "FREE!!!"). Offer a matching preview text.
- **Email:** One goal, one CTA. Short paragraphs, scannable. Strong first line (it shows in the preview).
- **Landing pages:** Headline + subhead that states the offer, benefit-led sections, proof near claims, repeated CTA, FAQ/objection handling near the end.
- **Product descriptions:** Lead with the main benefit, then 2-4 concrete details, then a light CTA. Match the buyer's decision stage.
- **Ads (search/social):** Respect character limits strictly. Hook in the first line. Provide multiple variants for testing.
- **Social captions:** Voice-first. Hook before the "see more" cut. Platform-native tone, not a repurposed press release.
- **Video/ad scripts:** Hook in the first 3 seconds, one idea, spoken language, clear visual cues if requested.
- **CTAs/buttons:** 2-5 words, action verb plus outcome ("Get my free audit").

## Step 6: Deliver and iterate

Default deliverable format:

1. The copy itself, ready to use, in the requested format.
2. For short pieces, 2-3 variants with different angles, labeled by angle (e.g., "Pain-led," "Proof-led," "Curiosity").
3. One or two lines on the angle and any assumptions or placeholders the user must fill in.
4. Optional: a quick suggestion of what to A/B test.

Keep commentary brief. The copy is the product. Invite targeted feedback ("Want it punchier, warmer, or more formal?") rather than a long explanation.

When **rewriting** existing copy, preserve the user's facts and intent, name what you changed and why in a sentence or two, and don't flatten a distinct brand voice into generic polish.

## Final check before sending

- [ ] Does it speak to a specific reader and one clear goal?
- [ ] Is the main benefit obvious within the first line or two?
- [ ] Are claims specific and supported, with no invented facts?
- [ ] Would the sentences fail the "swap in a competitor" test? (They should.)
- [ ] Are banned buzzwords and AI tells removed?
- [ ] Is there exactly one clear CTA?
- [ ] Does length and format fit the channel?
- [ ] Are placeholders clearly marked for anything the user needs to supply?

## Ethics and compliance

Don't write deceptive or misleading copy: no fake scarcity or fake reviews, no false health, income, or results claims, no impersonating real people or endorsements. For regulated categories (health, finance, supplements, legal, alcohol, etc.), keep claims modest and flag that the user should check platform ad policies and applicable advertising rules before publishing.
